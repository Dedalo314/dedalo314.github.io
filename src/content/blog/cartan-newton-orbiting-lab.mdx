Imagine an astronaut floating freely inside an orbiting laboratory, like the International Space Station. From a purely Newtonian viewpoint, both the station and the astronaut are falling together, creating a sense of weightlessness. But what if the lab is rotating to keep one side facing the Earth so that they can fall sleep with the views? How do we describe the subtle, seemingly invisible forces that the astronaut experiences is he gets away from the center of the lab?

Instead of relying on the standard, often clunky, Newtonian kinematics of rotating frames, we can use the elegant language of differential geometry. By treating and the lab's center of mass and the astronaut as two nearby particles moving along their respective worldlines, their relative motion is perfectly described by the **Geodesic Deviation Equation**:

$$\frac{D^2 n^\alpha}{d\lambda^2} + R^\alpha_{\beta\gamma\delta} \frac{dx^\beta}{d\lambda} n^\gamma \frac{dx^\delta}{d\lambda} = 0$$

In this post, we will unpack this equation step-by-step. We'll see how the mathematical machinery of covariant derivatives (the connection coefficients) and spacetime curvature (the Riemann tensor) naturally spit out the familiar fictitious forces—Coriolis and Centrifugal—without us having to add them by hand. Then, we will visualize the surprising orbital mechanics that emerge from these equations.

---

## 1. The Setup: Inertial vs. Rotating Frames

To understand the relative motion, we must define two coordinate systems:

- $(x, y, z)$: The strictly inertial frame (Earth-centered).
- $(x', y', z')$: The rotating frame (lab-centered), where the $x'$-axis always points radially away from the Earth.

The coordinate transformation between the inertial Earth-centered frame $\mathbf{x}$ and the rotating lab-centered frame $\mathbf{x}'$ is defined by a translation $\mathbf{R}(t)$ and a rotation $\mathbf{A}(t)$:

$$\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{R}(t) + \mathbf{A}(t) \mathbf{x}'$$

Where:

- $\mathbf{R}(t) = \begin{pmatrix} R \cos(\omega t) \\ R \sin(\omega t) \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ tracks the lab's orbit.
- $\mathbf{A}(t) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(\omega t) & -\sin(\omega t) & 0 \\ \sin(\omega t) & \cos(\omega t) & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ rotates the local axes at orbital angular velocity $\omega$.

In index notation, this is cleanly written as:

$$x^i = R^i(t) + A^i_{\ j'}(t) x^{j'}$$.

---

## 2. Deriving the Connection Coefficients ($\Gamma$)

Because our local frame $(x', y', z')$ is rotating, it is a non-inertial frame. In differential geometry, non-inertial frames manifest as non-zero connection coefficients (Christoffel symbols, $\Gamma$).

When moving from the flat, inertial frame (where $\Gamma = 0$) to our rotating frame, we must use the transformation rule for connections:

$$\Gamma^{i'}_{j'k'} = \langle \sigma^{i'}, \nabla_{k'}\mathbf{e}_{j'} \rangle = \frac{\partial x^{i'}}{\partial x^\alpha} \frac{\partial^2 x^\alpha}{\partial x^{j'} \partial x^{k'}}$$

where $\mathbf{\sigma}^{i'}$ is the dual one-form of the coordinate basis $\mathbf{e}_{j'}$: $\langle \mathbf{\sigma}^{i'}, \mathbf{e}_{j'} \rangle = \delta^{i'}_{j'}$.

Since in Cartan-Newton spacetime time is universal ($t = x^{0'}$) and each space slice is flat, the spatial derivatives vanish, leaving only the mixed space-time components $\Gamma^{i'}_{j'0'}$ and the time component $\Gamma^{i'}_{0'0'}$ non-zero. By applying the inverse rotation matrix and differentiating $\mathbf{A}(t)$ with respect to time, the matrix multiplication $\mathbf{A}^{-1} \dot{\mathbf{A}}$ yields the skew-symmetric angular velocity matrix.

Then, for a rotation around the z-axis, the calculation gives exactly:

$$\Gamma^{1'}_{2'0'} = -\omega, \quad \Gamma^{2'}_{1'0'} = \omega,  \quad \Gamma^{i'}_{0'0'} = 0$$.

These coefficients are the geometric fingerprint of rotation.

---

## 3. The First Covariant Derivative: Relative Velocity

Now, let's look at the astronaut. We define a deviation vector $n^{\alpha'}$ pointing from the lab's center to the astronaut. Since time is universal and $t = \lambda$, $n^{0'} = \left(\frac{\partial t}{\partial n^{0'}}\right)_\lambda = 0$, leaving only the spatial distance $n^{i'}$.

The lab is at rest in its own frame, moving purely through time, so its 4-velocity is $u^{\mu'} = \frac{dx^{\mu'}}{dt} = (1, 0, 0, 0)$.

To find the geometric relative velocity, we take the covariant derivative of the deviation vector along the lab's worldline ($\lambda = t$):

$$\frac{Dn^{i'}}{dt} = \frac{dn^{i'}}{dt} + \Gamma^{i'}_{\beta'\mu'} n^{\beta'} \frac{dx^{\mu'}}{dt}$$

Since $\mu' = 0'$ and $\beta' = j'$, this simplifies instantly to:

$$\frac{Dn^{i'}}{dt} = \frac{dn^{i'}}{dt} + \Gamma^{i'}_{j'0'} n^{j'}$$.

Physically, this is profound. The "true" geometric velocity is the apparent velocity seen by the astronaut ($\frac{dn^{i'}}{dt}$) _plus_ a rotational drag term ($\Gamma^{i'}_{j'0'} n^{j'}$), which perfectly maps to the classical $\vec{\omega} \times \vec{n}$ velocity addition.

---

## 4. The Second Covariant Derivative: Emergence of Coriolis

To get acceleration, we take the covariant derivative a second time:

$$\frac{D^2 n^{i'}}{dt^2} = \frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{Dn^{i'}}{dt} \right) + \Gamma^{i'}_{\beta'\mu'} \frac{Dn^{\beta'}}{dt} \frac{dx^{\mu'}}{dt}$$

Expanding this via the product rule and substituting our known $\Gamma$ values:

$$\frac{D^2 n^{i'}}{dt^2} = \frac{d^2 n^{i'}}{dt^2} + 2\omega \left[ \delta^{2'}_{i'} \frac{dn^{1'}}{dt} - \delta^{1'}_{i'} \frac{dn^{2'}}{dt} \right] - \omega^2\left[n^{1'}\delta^{1'}_{i'} + n^{2'}\delta^{2'}_{i'}\right]$$

Look closely at the terms generated by this geometric expansion.

The term multiplied by $2\omega$ contains alternating velocity components $\frac{dn^{i'}}{dt}$, which is the exact index-notation equivalent of **the classical Coriolis acceleration**, $2\vec{\omega} \times \dot{\vec{n}}$. Meanwhile, the term multiplied by $\omega^2$ scales directly with the distance $n$ from the origin, representing **the kinematic Centrifugal acceleration**, $-\vec{\omega} \times (\vec{\omega} \times \vec{n})$.

Without us having to manually paste in Newtonian corrections, the machinery of the second covariant derivative has naturally derived **both the Coriolis and Centrifugal accelerations purely from the geometric twist of our rotating coordinate system**!

---

## 5. Obtaining the Curvature Term ($R'$): Tidal Gravity

The second half of the Geodesic Deviation Equation relies on the **Riemann curvature tensor**. It describes the actual tidal gravity of the Earth. In Newton-Cartan, all $R^{\alpha}_{\beta\gamma\delta}$ vanish but $R^{j}_{0k0} = - R^{j}_{00k} = \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x^j \partial x^k}$.

To find $R^{i'}_{0'j'0'}$ in our rotating lab, we can make use of the geometric, abstract equation for $\mathbf{Riemann(...)}$. $\mathbf{Riemann(...)}$ is a geometric object, so it has physical meaning independently of our coordinate system, and the abstract equation will be valid even in our non-intertial, rotating lab. Let's find out the geometric equation in the Earth's inertial frame and then translate the equation to our coordinates in the lab's frame!

From Newton we know that (normalized constants so $G = 1$):

$$\phi = \frac{-GM}{r} = - \frac{M}{r}$$.

And carefully taking the derivatives $\partial_i \partial_j \phi$:

$$R^{j}_{0k0} = - R^{j}_{00k} = \frac{M}{r^3}\left[ \delta^{j}_{k} - 3\frac{x^k x^j}{r^2} \right]$$,

where $r = \left( \sum_i x^i x^i \right)^{1/2}$.

Now, we can infer the abstract spacetime equation that leads to the coordinate equation by promoting to tensors:

$$\mathbf{R} = \frac{M}{r^3}\left( \mathbf{P} - 3 \mathbf{\hat{r}}\otimes \mathbf{\hat{r}} \right)\mathbf{d}t\mathbf{d}t$$,

where $\mathbf{\hat{r}} = \mathbf{x}_{lab center} / r$ is a unit vector pointing radially outward from the source and $\mathbf{P}$ is the spatial projection matrix (diagonal matrix, identity in space and 0 in time).

Again, as this is a geometric, abstract notation spacetime equation, it must work in the lab's coordinates $x^{\alpha'}$ too! And as $x^{1'}$ always points outwards radially due to the rotation of the lab, $\mathbf{\hat{r}'} = \left(0,1,0,0 \right)$ (easy right?). Then we get:

$$R^{j'}_{0'k'0'} = - R^{j'}_{0'0'k'} = \frac{M}{r^3}\begin{pmatrix} -2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \omega^2\begin{pmatrix} -2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$,

and $R^{\alpha'}_{\beta'\gamma'\delta'} = 0$ elsewhere (coherent with Cartan-Newton geometry).

Finally, the term for the Geodesic Deviation Equation results in

$$R^{i'}_{0'j'0'} n^{j'} = \omega^2 \left[-2 n^{1'}\delta^{i'}_{1'} + n^{2'} \delta^{i'}_{2'} + n^{3'} \delta^{i'}_{3'} \right]$$.

This expression, proportional to $\omega^2$ and the deviation position $\mathbf{n}'$, represents the true gravitational tidal forces of the Earth. Instead of a fictitious artifact of rotation, this curvature term captures the real physical stretching and squeezing—the gravity gradient—caused by the fact that the astronaut is at a slightly different point in Earth's gravity well than the lab's center of mass.

---

## 6. The Geodesic Deviation Equations of Motion

Putting it all together, the geodesic deviation equation, when projected onto our rotating local frame, yields the following coupled system of second-order differential equations:

$$\frac{d^2 n^{1'}}{dt^2} - 2\omega \frac{dn^{2'}}{dt} - 3\omega^2 n^{1'} = 0$$,

$$\frac{d^2 n^{2'}}{dt^2} + 2\omega \frac{dn^{1'}}{dt}= 0$$,

$$\frac{d^2 n^{3'}}{dt^2} + \omega^2 n^{3'} = 0$$.

These are equations governing the motion of the astronaut relative to the lab's center of mass, if slightly pushed (locally). In orbital mechanics, these are famously known as the **Clohessy-Wiltshire** (or Hill's) equations.

---

## 7. The Boomerang Effect: Proving the Oscillation

If you throw an object (or the astronaut) purely radially outward ($+x'$ direction), you might expect it to float away forever. Instead, the object loops backward and eventually returns exactly to your hand. We can prove this mathematically.

Let's assume initial conditions: $x'(0) = 0$, $y'(0) = 0$, $\dot{x'}(0) = v_0$, and $\dot{y'}(0) = 0$. As $\mathbf{n'}$ is the separation vector from the lab's center (fidutial geodesic), spatially it's the same as the position of the object in $x^{i'}$ coordinates (not in time-dimension, t vs 0, but time-dimension is not involved due to Cartan-Newton geometry!).

If we integrate the second equation once, we get:
$$\dot{y'} = -2\omega x$$.

Substitute this back into the first equation:

$$\ddot{x'} = 2\omega(-2\omega x') + 3\omega^2 x' \Rightarrow \ddot{x'} = -4\omega^2 x' + 3\omega^2 x' \Rightarrow \ddot{x'} + \omega^2 x' = 0$$.

The fictitious forces perfectly collapse into a **simple harmonic oscillator** with frequency $\omega$. Solving this system yields a closed, periodic ellipse:

$$x'(t) = \frac{v_0}{\omega}\sin(\omega t)$$

$$y'(t) = \frac{2v_0}{\omega}(\cos(\omega t) - 1)$$.

And from the third equation, it's trivial that $z'(t) = 0$. As expected the object stays in the X'Y'-plane.

Finally, note that when the clock hits one orbital period ($t = \frac{2\pi}{\omega}$), $\sin(2\pi) = 0$ and $\cos(2\pi) = 1$. The object returns exactly to $(0,0)$!.

### The Inertial Perspective

Why does this happen? In the Earth-centered inertial frame, your outward throw doesn't change the object's tangential speed, but it pushes it into a **slightly elliptical orbit**. Because the energy of an orbit depends almost entirely on its tangential velocity, this new elliptical orbit has the _exact same orbital period_ as the lab's circular orbit. Two intersecting orbits with the exact same period are guaranteed to meet again after one full rotation!

### The Rotating Perspective

Inside the lab, the outward drift ($+x$) triggers a relentless Coriolis force ($2\omega v_y$ and $-2\omega v_x$). This acts like a magnetic field, constantly bending the object's path to the right ($-y$ direction), halting its outward progress, and whipping it back toward the center.

## 9. Solving with SymPy

<Collapsible title="Show SymPy Code">

```python
import sympy as sp

t = sp.symbols('t')
n1 = sp.Function('n1')(t)
n2 = sp.Function('n2')(t)
n3 = sp.Function('n3')(t)
omega = sp.symbols('omega', real=True)

# ẍ = 2ωẏ + 3ω²x
# ÿ = -2ωẋ
# ẑ = -ω²z

eq1 = sp.Eq(n1.diff(t, 2), 2 * omega * n2.diff(t) + 3 * omega**2 * n1)
eq2 = sp.Eq(n2.diff(t, 2), -2 * omega * n1.diff(t))
eq3 = sp.Eq(n3.diff(t, 2), -omega**2 * n3)

sol = sp.dsolve((eq1, eq2, eq3))
print(sp.latex(sol))
```

</Collapsible>

The analytical solution, obtained via SymPy, is:

$$\begin{cases} n^{1'}(t) = -\frac{2 C_1}{3 \omega} + \frac{C_2 \sin(\omega t)}{2 \omega} - \frac{C_3 \cos(\omega t)}{2 \omega} \\ n^{2'}(t) = C_1 t + \frac{C_2 \cos(\omega t)}{\omega} + \frac{C_3 \sin(\omega t)}{\omega} + C_4 \\ n^{3'}(t) = \frac{C_5 \sin(\omega t)}{\omega} + \frac{C_6 \cos(\omega t)}{\omega} \end{cases}$$

This solution shows that motion in the $n^{1'}n^{2'}$-plane (radial and tangential) is coupled and leads to a drift (the $C_1 t$ term), while motion in the $n^{3'}$ (vertical) direction is a simple harmonic oscillator.

## The Takeaway

What the "Orbiting Lab" exercise beautifully demonstrates is that fictitious forces are not ad-hoc corrections we must paste onto Newton's laws. By simply transforming our coordinates and demanding that geometry remains consistent, the machinery of covariant derivatives and the Riemann tensor automatically generates the Coriolis and Centrifugal effects.
